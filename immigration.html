Immigration matters we can talk with you about

Ask during your consultation whether we can take your case. Every case is quoted in writing after a consultation.

Family petitions

For U.S. citizens who want to bring a spouse, child, parent, brother, or sister to live in the United States. You must be 21 or older to petition for a parent, brother, or sister. Permanent residents can petition for a spouse or unmarried child (Form I-130).

Green card from inside the U.S.

Applying for permanent residence without leaving the country, when the law allows it (adjustment of status, Form I-485), often with a work permit and travel document request.

Removing conditions on a green card

For people with a two-year green card through marriage. A couple filing together must file within the 90 days before the card expires (Form I-751).

Green card renewal or replacement

Renewing an expiring 10-year green card, or replacing one that was lost, stolen, damaged, or has wrong information (Form I-90).

Citizenship

Applying to become a U.S. citizen through naturalization (Form N-400), including a review of your history before you file.

Work permits

First-time and renewal work permit requests when you qualify (Form I-765).

Immigration court

Cases in immigration court, including bond hearings. We look at each one case by case after the consultation and tell you whether we can take it.

Have these nearby during your appointment:

  • Your passport or other photo ID (for example, a driver's license or state ID).
  • Your green card, work permit, or other immigration card, if you have one.
  • Any notice you've received from USCIS, such as a receipt or approval notice (Form I-797).
  • Any papers from an immigration court, immigration officers, or the border.
  • Copies of applications or petitions you or a relative filed before.
  • Birth and marriage certificates, and any divorce decrees.
  • Any arrest, charge, or court records, even if the case was dismissed or expunged.

USCIS requires a full English translation of any document in another language. The translator must sign a statement that the translation is complete and accurate and that they are competent to translate. We do not provide translation services.

Common questions

Do I have to come to your office?

No. Our immigration appointments are held by video. USCIS may still ask you to go to one of its offices in person, for example for fingerprints or an interview. USCIS requires lawyers to attend interviews at its offices in person, so ask us during your consultation whether we can attend yours.

Can you promise my case will be approved?

No. No lawyer can promise how USCIS, a consulate, or an immigration court will decide a case. The outcome of any matter depends on its own facts.

Can I file immigration forms myself?

Yes. USCIS says you do not need a representative to file forms. If you want help, use a licensed attorney or an accredited representative. USCIS forms are available at uscis.gov/forms.

Is a notario or immigration consultant the same as a lawyer?

No. In some countries a "notario público" is a lawyer. In the United States, a notary public is not a lawyer and cannot give you immigration legal advice. Only licensed attorneys and accredited representatives of organizations recognized by the U.S. Department of Justice may give immigration legal advice or represent you. Learn how to spot scams at USCIS: Avoid Scams.

Can a Tennessee lawyer help with immigration?

Yes. Immigration law is federal law, so a lawyer licensed in any U.S. state can represent people before USCIS, and, once registered with EOIR, before the immigration courts. Does contacting you make me a client?

No. Calling, emailing, or having a consultation does not create an attorney-client relationship. A relationship is formed only after we agree in a signed written agreement to represent you. Until then, we are not responsible for any deadline in your matter.

Someone contacted me saying they're from USCIS and asked for money. What should I do?

Be careful. Scammers pretend to be government officials. They may use social media, email, or phone calls. USCIS warns about emails that don't end in .gov, offers to speed up a case for money, and messages claiming to be from USCIS that ask you to send money to a person or pay fees outside your USCIS online account. See USCIS: Avoid Scams. You can report a scam to the Federal Trade Commission at reportfraud.ftc.gov.
